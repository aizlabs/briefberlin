Germany urgently needs doctors from abroad. But people who studied outside the EU often wait years for their qualifications to be recognised. At the end of 2025, a quarter of all doctors here were 60 or older; among doctors with their own practices, the figure was 44 percent. Appointments with specialists are already hard to get, and general practices are overstretched. The number of dental practices is also expected to fall soon.

One example is the Syrian dentist Ghassan Al-Shalak. He came to Berlin in 2015 but was not allowed to work fully until five years later. In the meantime, the authorities did not renew his professional permit, even though he had employment contracts. As a result, he was only allowed to stay temporarily without a secure residence status. Today he has his own practice in Kreuzberg.

There are two routes to recognition. Either experts check the certificates, or applicants take a three-part exam directly. But the document route costs hundreds of euros for translations alone and often takes months or years. The dentist Zeynep Çınar from Istanbul submitted her documents in Bavaria in January 2024. Only with the help of a lawyer did she learn that documents were missing, and in March 2026 her application was rejected. She has paid around 7,000 euros, plus about 1,300 euros in exam fees. Strangely, Baden-Württemberg found far more differences in the case of a friend who had completed exactly the same course of study. The authorities examine each case individually, but the Marburger Bund, a doctors’ union, is calling for a central authority.

Even for the exam, candidates in Baden-Württemberg wait ten to 18 months for a date, although the law provides for six months. In Rhineland-Palatinate, the wait is 15 months. A new law is meant to speed everything up from November. Already, about 80 percent choose the exam. People who do not get a new permit after two years often take jobs at snack bars or as taxi drivers. Meanwhile, agents and advisers are also making money from the complicated procedures.

This could prove costly for Germany. In a survey, many doctors who wanted to emigrate did not want to go to Germany. Of these, 44 percent preferred a country with simpler recognition procedures. Some are also considering leaving because of the right-wing populist climate. Yet almost 8,000 doctors from Syria alone work here without German citizenship.
