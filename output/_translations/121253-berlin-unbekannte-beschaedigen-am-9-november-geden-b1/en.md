On the anniversary of the November pogroms of 1938, unknown people damaged several memorial sites in Berlin for victims of the Nazis. The Recherche- und Informationsstelle Antisemitismus Berlin (RIAS) recorded three cases around November 9. Stumbling stones in Mitte and on Kottbusser Damm in Kreuzberg were affected. A poster on an advertising column at Hermannplatz in Neukölln was also damaged. Project leader Julia Kopp is alarmed. She thinks it is cynical that perpetrators deliberately choose memorial days for such acts.

The poster commemorates the deportations of Berlin’s Jews 85 years ago. It shows a photo of Arno Althoff. Unknown people scratched out his eyes and put stickers in the colors of the Palestinian flag over his eyes and mouth. Althoff was born in Berlin in 1919. His parents lost their business in Neukölln, and he was forced to work. In 1942, he left a safe hiding place because he wanted to say goodbye to his parents. After his deportation to Riga, he disappeared without a trace. In Mitte, perpetrators made a stumbling stone unreadable with ketchup and destroyed a bouquet of roses. On Kottbusser Damm, unknown people painted white lines across the stones for the Israel family on the night of November 10. In front of them, they wrote “Palastine”.

RIAS sees these as targeted attacks on remembrance. Memorial sites show people in the city how the Nazis persecuted and murdered European Jews. Anyone who damages them is therefore attacking the victims of the Shoah, or Holocaust, Kopp explains. She calls this antisemitism after the Shoah. Some people openly deny the crimes. Others reject any sense of guilt, mock the victims or downplay the crimes. At present, many people use the Shoah to portray Israel and Zionism as enemies. Anyone who equates Israel with Nazism reverses the roles of perpetrators and victims.

The new acts are part of a longer trend. In 2025, RIAS counted 61 antisemitic incidents at memorial sites in Berlin. Since then, reports have come in from almost all districts. Most involved deliberate damage, often with comparisons to the war in Gaza. Stumbling stones are small brass plaques in the pavement. They give the names and life dates of people who were persecuted and murdered. Incidents can be reported to RIAS, even if they are not criminal offenses. People affected can receive advice there if they wish.
