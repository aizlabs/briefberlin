Conflict over the seat
Berlin's Die Linke has intensified its dispute with Vanessa Emde from Neukölln. On Friday evening, the Berlin party leadership called on her to give up her seat in the Abgeordnetenhaus. That is Berlin's state parliament. It spoke of a serious loss of trust.

Accusations from the party
Die Linke says: Emde has broken joint decisions several times. She also did not keep an agreement for elected members of parliament. She had signed it with the party before the election. It says that members of parliament should respect the party program and election program.

Dispute over Israel
The trigger was several public statements by Emde. Critics in the party and possible government partners understood them as doubts about Israel's right to exist as a state. Emde also supported the activist Ibrahim Ibrahim. Security authorities see him as close to Hamas. Hamas is an Islamist organization that attacks Israel. The case became especially sensitive after a statement against antisemitism. Emde had supported this statement. Shortly afterward, she published a video. Critics saw it as a contradiction of the statement. Her protest against her own party leadership at a Die Linke party conference also intensified the dispute.

Consequences for talks
The affair at times put a strain on talks with the Greens and SPD about a possible government. Such first talks are called exploratory talks. Representatives of both parties demanded clear consequences in the Emde case. Therefore the talks paused briefly. Later, however, the parties completed them. In Die Linke, Emde is also seen as an opponent of taking part in government. Her invitation of the anti-Israel activist Hannah Bruns to a parliamentary group meeting caused further anger. An application for Bruns's expulsion from the party is already under way.

What happens next
It is unclear whether Emde will give up her seat. Many in the party do not expect that. She recently said: She is not taking back any of her controversial statements. If she keeps the seat, the parliamentary group leadership can apply to exclude her from the Die Linke parliamentary group. For that, it needs two thirds of the votes. This majority is now seen as possible. Katalin Gennburg and Gregor Gysi are calling for a separation. Emde's supporters, however, see this as giving in to public pressure. They accuse the party leadership of giving up its own principles for a possible coalition.
