In many large cities in Germany, the housing crisis today is more than a social problem. Rents are rising, building land is scarce, and having an apartment of one’s own remains out of reach for many people. That is why politicians, cities, the construction industry, and social groups are looking for new alliances. They are meant to find solutions more quickly and ease the housing market.

The crisis reaches deep into private decisions. Some couples postpone starting a family because they cannot find a larger affordable apartment. Others stay in apartments that are too small, even though their lives have changed. Moving for a new job is also becoming more difficult. This especially affects successful regions where many companies need new employees. Anyone who wants to work there often cannot find an affordable place to stay.

In the past, owning a home was seen by many families as a sign of social advancement. Today, many people are losing faith in this promise. When even good incomes are no longer enough, insecurity grows. Companies also feel the consequences. They find fewer workers when apartments nearby are too expensive. The housing issue is therefore important not only privately, but also economically.

The longer affordable apartments are lacking, the greater the political pressure becomes. Parties and initiatives are therefore calling for stronger interventions in the market. One especially radical proposal is the transfer of large housing holdings into public control. This means that large housing companies should no longer control their apartments solely as private property. Instead, society, meaning the state or a public body, should have more say. Many people hope this will lead to lower rents. But in the short term, this does not create a single new apartment.

This is exactly where new alliances come in. When administrations, construction companies, tenants’ associations, and owners work together, they can clarify conflicts earlier. They can also plan better where new apartments make sense. Such cooperation does not solve the crisis immediately. Even so, it can help regain trust and make concrete construction projects possible more quickly. Without new affordable apartments, the pressure will continue to grow.
