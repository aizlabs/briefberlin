The cyberattack on Berlin’s state administration has had greater consequences than previously known. Last August, criminals stole around 5.79 terabytes of data from the Senate departments for transport and construction. After a deadline passed, they published the data on the dark web, a hidden part of the internet. The data includes 9,708 applications for rent subsidies for social housing from 2016 to early 2020. There are also 2,824 follow-up applications and 1,667 applications to change existing claims. Because partners, children and other household members are also named, a total of 16,858 people are affected. This is probably almost everyone who applied during that period.

The names, dates of birth and addresses of almost all applicants are visible. For two thirds, it is also possible to see their marital status, rent and household income. The data also includes bank details for around 4,000 people, about 6,400 telephone numbers and over 700 email addresses. For 2,423 people, there is information about a severe disability and, in some cases, a need for long-term care. Particularly sensitive are text fields in which people had to explain why their home was larger than the law allows. Many describe separations, depression, miscarriages, deaths or their children’s disabilities. The texts also show how difficult the situation is in Berlin’s housing market. One single man, for example, fears becoming homeless without his home.

Apparently, a single email was the cause of the problem. In early 2020, Investitionsbank Berlin-Brandenburg took over the processing of the applications from another company. At the time, a courier brought the data to the bank on external hard drives. A few weeks later, an employee of the Senatsverwaltung für Stadtentwicklung und Wohnen sent three Excel spreadsheets containing the data to a colleague. The colleague worked in the same building, and it is still unclear why he received the data. At first, it was also unclear whether the Senatskanzlei knew the full extent of the problem and had already informed those affected.

The case will probably have legal consequences. Experts see it as a serious violation of the General Data Protection Regulation, the EU law protecting personal data. A Berlin data protection lawyer believes that those affected can claim several thousand euros in compensation. This applies to each household member individually, so families can quickly reach more than 10,000 euros. The more sensitive the data, the higher the claim. Those affected can also join together and take legal action. Even so, money can probably do little to make up for the loss of privacy.
