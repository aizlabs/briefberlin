On Saturday, the fourth world championship in “Don't Get Angry” took place at a Berlin hotel near Gleisdreieck. A total of 172 players sat at 43 tables. Most came from Germany, with some from the Netherlands, Austria and Switzerland. The youngest participant was seven, the oldest nearly 90. But the largest group were baby boomers.

The game has been found in almost every German household for over a hundred years. The company Schmidt Spiele brought it to market in 1915. It estimates that it has sold around 100 million copies so far. About 400,000 more are sold each year, and the number is rising. Young people also enjoy playing games away from screens, as a break from them.

The tournament was run much like a chess competition. In four preliminary rounds, with 30 minutes of playing time at first, everyone collected points for the final rounds. The four best players then competed for the title in the final. According to the organizers, luck matters most, and there is hardly any way to train for the game. Even so, a clever strategy is needed. Organizer Axel Kaldenhoven, 62, is the managing director of Schmidt Spiele in Berlin. For him, contact between people matters most. Unlike in digital games, players can see each other's faces and gestures directly. It is a competition, but a friendly one.

Many participants agreed. Christel Haase, 77, from Potsdam played on the “Speedy Dice” team. Like her teammates, she wore a cardboard die in her hair. She particularly enjoys the conversations and the mental exercise. She doesn't need to win, but she is still ambitious.

In the end, a well-known favorite won: Mandy Kunze, 50, from Thiendorf in Saxony. She was world champion at the same venue two years ago and German champion twice in a row. Thiendorf has its own club with 20 to 25 members and is considered a center of the game. Because of her green club shirt, the others sometimes take special pleasure in attacking her, Kunze says. Her tactic is to sneak a piece past the others as quietly as possible. It worked very well in the final. After about 30 minutes, her last piece had reached the finish, while the others had only got one or two pieces that far. Kunze received a trophy and a travel voucher worth 250 euros. The other finalists, including 84-year-old Berlin resident Dagmar Marten, received vouchers for games.
